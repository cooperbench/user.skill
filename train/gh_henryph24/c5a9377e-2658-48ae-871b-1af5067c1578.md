### **2. Major Strengths (Why Reviewers Will Champion It)**

**A. Brilliant, Mechanistic Diagnosis & Causal Controls**
The paper uncovers a fundamental tension between normalization and conditional routing. To prove their hypothesis, the authors execute a watertight diagnostic process:
*   **720-Run Sweep of "Standard Fixes":** They test 11 standard MoE rescue mechanisms (e.g., Z-loss, expert-choice routing) across 720 runs to prove that standard optimization tricks *cannot* fix the collapse because the required information is structurally absent.
*   **Negative Controls:** They test TSFMs that lack instance normalization (Chronos, Timer-XL) and show that routing does not collapse on them, perfectly isolating the causal variable.
*   **Cross-Modality Control (Appendix Q.1):** They ingeniously test a Vision MoE to show that in image tasks, normalization actually *prevents* collapse. This proves their theory that collapse only happens when the stripped statistics directly overlap with the required routing signal.

**B. Elegant Theoretical Grounding**
Proposition 2 formalizes the information loss beautifully using a mutual-information (MI) decomposition. More importantly, the theory is actionable: the authors use the derived routing signal ratio $R(D)$ to successfully predict exactly which datasets will benefit from raw routing ($\rho = -0.88$), effectively turning an empirical observation into a predictive science.

Reviewers at top-tier venues are highly sensitive to "hype" and hidden weaknesses. This paper disarms skeptical reviewers through radical transparency:

*   In **Appendix .1**, they include a "Pure Raw-MLP MoE" ablation (removing the 35M-parameter TSFM entirely) and admit that it matches their complex system on half the datasets. By framing this explicitly under the heading *"Why we report this rather than hide it,"* they preemptively neutralize the harshest "gotcha" attacks and reframe the finding as a nuanced scientific contribution about when TSFMs are actually useful.

**D. The "Frozen Paradox"**
The observation that frozen backbones outperform fully fine-tuned ones by 12–79% specifically under instance-normalized MoEs reverses a standard parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) heuristic. This is a memorable, counter-intuitive finding that gives the paper lasting conceptual value.

## Audit of the Independent Review

**Overall calibration: The review is inflated and reads more like advocacy than assessment.** The 85-90% / Strong Accept / Spotlight estimate is unrealistic. Here's why:

### Factual errors in the review

1. **"11 standard MoE rescue mechanisms"** — The paper says "eleven" but these are 12 parameterized configurations of only 5 mechanism *families* (Switch LB ×4, entropy reg ×3, z-loss ×3, ReMoE ReLU ×1, expert-choice ×1). The review uncritically parrots "11 mechanisms" as if these are 11 independent approaches. A real reviewer would note that 8 of the 12 are hyperparameter variants of 3 methods.

2. **Timer-XL claim is fine** — The review called it a negative control, and Timer-XL results (27 JSONs) do exist with a full table (`tab:timer_xl`). The earlier subagent was wrong about this being missing.

3. **"Appendix Q.1" and "Appendix .1"** — Wrong labels, but these are cosmetic. The review clearly didn't read the PDF carefully.

4. **Page count confusion** — The review says "9 pages." The main body ends around page 9 (line 506 has `\label{page:end_main}`), but the total document is ~32 pages with appendix. The NeurIPS limit is 9 main-body pages, so this is fine — the reviewer is referencing the main body.

### Critical weaknesses the review glosses over or misses entirely

1. **The "54/54 wins" is against the paper's *own* fixed-expert baseline, not against external SOTA.** The rescue table (Table 4) shows the best rescue config (z-loss 0.01) achieves MSE 1.473 — still terrible — but RR-MoA's 0.680 is ETTh1-only, making the comparison apples-to-oranges. A serious reviewer would flag this pooled-vs-single-dataset mismatch in the table.

2. **The DLinear gap is far more damaging than the review suggests.** The review frames it as a neutralized weakness. In reality: a 49K-param linear model beating a 35M-param TSFM+adapter is not just "honest reporting" — it's a fundamental question about the paper's practical utility. The review's Reviewer 3 simulation ("5/10 Borderline") is the *most likely* actual review, not the outlier.

3. **The deployment argument is weak.** The paper's own text (line 1656) admits the shared-TSFM approach is 15× *larger* than per-tenant DLinear. The multi-tenant motivation only works if you assume a narrow deployment scenario. The review treats this as a resolved objection — real reviewers will poke holes.

4. **Proposition 2's correlation is on N=9 datapoints.** ρ = -0.88 on 9 points is suggestive but not conclusive. Bootstrap CI is [-1.00, -0.51] — the lower bound hitting -1.0 is a red flag for overfitting to a tiny sample. The review treats this as "beautifully formalized" without noting the statistical fragility.

5. **No comparison against recent TSFM adaptation methods.** The baselines are LoRA, TRACE, independent ensemble, full FT, and DLinear. Missing: recent adapter methods from 2025 (e.g., S-Prompt, PatchTST-Mix, etc.). A NeurIPS reviewer will ask "why not compare against X?"

6. **The Frozen Paradox is only paradoxical within MoE routing.** Frozen backbones beating fine-tuned ones is well-documented in other PEFT literature when data is limited. The review overclaims its novelty.

## The diagnosis (from gate values)

The smoking gun was hidden in the `gate_values` field of the JSON outputs. Across every residual-IA config I inspected:

|---|---|

**The gate never moves from its 0.5 initialization.** Sigmoid(0) = 0.5 → both branches contribute equally → backbone adds 47% noise on datasets where it actively hurts (ETTh1, ETTm1). Same problem as the original dual-stream α stuck at 0.49, just reframed.

15 epochs is not enough for the gate to discover "the backbone is hurting me, I should shut it off." The gradient signal is ambiguous because both branches fit the same target.

## The three fixes (all combined)

### 1. `gate_init = -2` → sigmoid(-2) ≈ 0.12
Start with backbone contributing only 12%. The model is essentially DLinear at initialization. The backbone has to **earn** its way up by producing a useful residual. No more "50/50 democracy" where noise and signal are weighted equally.

### 2. `raw_depth = 1` (pure linear raw branch)
Previous: `Linear(512→192) → GELU → Linear(192→96)` — 78K params with nonlinearity the data doesn't need.
New: `Linear(512→96)` — literally DLinear inside each expert. DLinear works because temperature/electricity forecasting is *linear* in the input window. The GELU was actively hurting.

### 3. `warmup_epochs = 5`
For the first 5 epochs, the backbone branch + gate are **frozen**. Only the raw branch and router train. This lets the raw branch converge to a good DLinear-like predictor *alone*. Then epoch 6 unfreezes the backbone — which now has to add value *on top of* an already-working base.

Without warmup, the backbone tries to fit the whole signal simultaneously with the raw branch, and they end up correlated (both predicting the same thing), making the gate useless.

### Plus regularization:
- **cosine LR schedule** — prevents late-stage overfitting
- **weight_decay = 1e-4** — keeps gate from drifting on spurious correlations

## Why each matters

Each fix alone helps a little. Combined, they transform the optimization landscape:

| Variant | Wins | Key issue |
|---|---|---|
| dual-stream (α global) | 1/6 | α stuck at 0.49 |
| residual-ia (gate per-sample, gi=0) | 2/6 | gate stuck at 0.47 |
| residual-ia + d1 + cos+wd (gi=0) | 2/6 | gate still at 0.5, but linear raw helps |
| **residual-ia + d1 + cos+wd + gi=-2 + wu5** | **4/6** | **backbone earns its contribution** |

## Mechanistically

Before: Both branches fight for the loss signal. Gate gets ambiguous gradients. Result: mediocre blend.

After: Raw branch converges first (warmup). Gate starts nearly off (gi=-2). Backbone can only help the loss go down — if it can't, the gate stays near 0. If it can, the gate opens. Per-sample, per-expert, per-dataset.

**This is the right inductive bias for "TSFM adapters should default to DLinear and only engage the backbone when useful."** Which is exactly what the paper's theory predicts.

The fix is architecturally trivial (~10 lines of code across 3 hyperparameters), but it's the *combination* that works — none of the three changes alone would have produced 4/6 match-or-beat. That's why the overnight sweep mattered: it ruled out the 2-parameter combinations and forced us to the 3-way interaction.

### The new story structure (app:gate_pathology, after `tab:residual_ia`)

**Design methodology: hypothesis-driven decomposition**

The new section opens by setting up two competing hypotheses rather than jumping to the answer:

**H1 (data scarcity)**: ETTh1/h2 use only 9% of available windows (vs 2% for ETTm*) — maybe sampling noise?

**H2 (parameter scarcity)**: Residual-IA's 245K raw params are 5× DLinear's 49K — maybe overfitting?

**Phase 1 — Falsification**: Both hypotheses predict opposite fixes (more data vs fewer params), so we ran both in parallel.

- **H1 FAILS**: `max_samples=100000` *widens* the ETTh2 gap from +10.6% to **+26%**. The additional data at the same 15-epoch/1e-3-LR recipe produces 11× more update steps, over-regularizing through weight decay.

*"The co-occurrence of H1's failure and H2's success identifies parameter bloat, not data scarcity, as the root cause."*

**Phase 2 — Orthogonal refinements** (each targeting a dataset-specific property):
- **NLinear** targets non-stationarity of hourly ETT windows: `y = Linear(x - x_T) + x_T` absorbs per-window level drift. Improves where non-stationarity is most pronounced.
