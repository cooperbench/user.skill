The toolkit at `/Users/user_c042661f/Documents/banodoco-workspace/tools/` is a set of file-based primitives. **Heavy mode — full hype-cut pipeline.** Many Gemini calls, produces one short condensed cut from a long source. Use when the task is "make a hype/sizzle/trailer." Covered in the [Pipeline](#pipeline) section onward.

**Light mode — compose primitives.** No Gemini, no `pipeline.py`, no render. ## Primitive recipes

Each recipe is a self-contained flow. None of them require the full pipeline; each calls one tool and adds a small amount of ffmpeg or Python.

### Transcript-driven chunking — split a long video at natural speech breaks

Use `transcribe.py` to get word/segment timestamps with silence boundaries, then ffmpeg-cut at the breaks closest to a target chunk length.

```bash
python3 tools/transcribe.py --audio source.mp4 --out work/
# work/transcript.json has segments with start/end timestamps
```

Then a short inline Python script: walk `transcript.json` segments, accumulate duration, and emit a cut whenever the running duration crosses your target AND the current segment ends near a silence (gap between this segment's `end` and next segment's `start` > ~0.5s). Drive `ffmpeg -ss S -to E -c copy out_NN.mp4` from the resulting list.

Stream copy (`-c copy`) is keyframe-aligned. If precise cuts matter, drop `-c copy` and re-encode with `-c:v libx264 -preset fast -crf 20 -c:a aac`.

### Scene-boundary splitting — one output file per detected scene

```bash
python3 tools/scenes.py --video source.mp4 --out work/
# work/scenes.json: [{index, start, end, duration}, ...]
```

Loop over `scenes.json` and ffmpeg-cut. Often you'll filter — drop scenes shorter than ~2s or merge adjacent ones under a minimum length — before emitting files.

### Mechanical chunking — fixed-length splits, no analysis

Skip the toolkit entirely. `ffmpeg -i source.mp4 -c copy -map 0 -segment_time 1200 -f segment -reset_timestamps 1 out_%03d.mp4` produces 20-minute pieces with no transcription or scene detection.

### Transcript-only / SRT-only

`transcribe.py` already emits `transcript.json`, `transcript.srt`, and `transcript.txt`. That's the whole job — no downstream stages needed.

### Keyframe extraction without a hype cut

`shots.py --per-scene N` produces evenly-spaced JPGs per detected scene. Useful for thumbnail grids, contact sheets, or feeding stills to other vision tools.

69	## 2. Architecture — the multi-LoRA inference stack
70	
71	The pack is built for a **stacked-LoRA** inference flow, not a single
72	all-purpose LoRA. One dataset produces all the data needed for several
73	LoRA types; each LoRA trains separately; at inference, the user composes
74	the ones they need for the task.
75	
76	### Why stacking instead of one mega-LoRA
77	
78	A single scene/character LoRA learns *identity, style, set, shot-type
79	vocabulary*. It cannot learn *transformations*:
80	
81	- Cross-shot **state continuity** ("Kramer was on the stool in the wide;
82	  on the cut back to wide, he's still on the stool")
83	- Cinematic **angle-pair cuts** (wide → over-shoulder of the same moment,
84	  with character state preserved)
85	- Smooth **scene transitions** (cut from one conversation moment to
86	  another)
87	
88	These are different transformations that share a base scene-LoRA but
89	need their own specialized weights.
90	
91	### LoRA types and what each needs
92	
93	| LoRA | Job | Conditioning | Pair shape | Dataset filter |
94	|---|---|---|---|---|
95	| **scene-LoRA** | identity, set, style, shot-type vocab | text only | single clip + caption | `provisional.manifest.json` |
96	| **angle-pair IC-LoRA** | image of moment M → new-angle 5s of same moment | last frame of prior shot | (image, video, caption) | `pairs.manifest.json` where `intra_scene=true` |
97	| **transition IC-LoRA** | smooth cinematic cut between scenes | last frame of clip N | (image, video, caption) | `pairs.manifest.json` where `intra_scene=false` |
98	| **continuation IC-LoRA** _(future)_ | extend a 5s clip into next 5s of same shot | last 8N+1 frames of clip A | (video tail, video, caption) | adjacent windows, same scene_idx |
99

1	# Seinfeld LoRA — Captioning Standard
2	
3	This is the load-bearing doc for how we caption training data. It encodes the
4	LTX 2.3 four-section format and the trigger-token-vs-anchor-phrase strategy.
5	If you change the captioning approach, change THIS file first, then update
6	`vocab_compile.py` and `dataset_build/run.py` to match.
7	
8	## Principle: match the model's training distribution
9	
10	LTX 2.3 was trained on a specific caption distribution. Departing from it
11	loses signal even if a different format "feels" cleaner. We follow the
12	official format even when it costs more pipeline complexity.
13	
14	## The four sections (verbatim, every caption)
15	
16	```
17	Scene: A {shot_type} shot inside {scene_token}. {character_anchors_assembled}.
18	       {camera}. {scene_description}
19	Speech: "{verbatim_quote}" — {speaker}, {tone}.
20	        ... (one line per spoken line, in order)
21	        (or the literal line "No spoken dialogue." when none)
22	Sounds: {laugh_track_clause}. {ambient}. {music_clause}.
23	Style: Seinfeld sitcom, 90s NBC multi-cam look, studio sitcom lighting.
24	```
25	
26	Concrete example:
27	
28	```
29	Scene: A wide shot inside jerrys_apt. George, a short stocky balding man with
30	       glasses, wearing a polo shirt and chinos. Jerry, a thin man in his
31	       thirties with short dark hair, wearing a button-down shirt and jeans.
32	       Static camera, medium-wide framing. George stands by the door while
33	       Jerry naps on the couch; George approaches and shakes his shoulder.
34	Speech: "Jerry. Jerry, wake up." — George, hushed and urgent.
35	        "What. What is it." — Jerry, drowsy and irritated.
36	Sounds: Studio audience laughter present after Jerry's line. Soft apartment
37	        ambience, a refrigerator hum. No music.
38	Style: Seinfeld sitcom, 90s NBC multi-cam look, studio sitcom lighting.
39	```
40	
41	## Why each section matters
42	
43	| Section | What it gives the LoRA |
44	|---|---|
45	| **Scene (visual)** | Discrete scene token (`jerrys_apt`/`monks_diner`) becomes the *select-a-location* control at inference. Character anchors repeated verbatim across the dataset become learned identifiers via repetition (the LTX/WaveSpeed "Tom Shelby" pattern). Camera + action describe motion the LoRA must reproduce. |
46	| **Speech (verbatim)** | LTX 2.3 generates audio as well as video. Verbatim quotes anchor dialogue rhythm, cadence, and tone — *the* defining feature of Seinfeld. Omitting them trains off-distribution from the base model's captioner. |
47	| **Sounds** | Laugh track is a Seinfeld-style fingerprint; the slap-bass between-scene cue is iconic. Capturing both lets the LoRA inherit them. Ambient is free-text so descriptive richness isn't bounded. |
48	| **Style (repeated)** | The literal `style_suffix` is appended to every caption verbatim. Repetition anchors the style without burning a discrete trigger-token slot. |
49	
50	## What we did NOT do, and why
51	
52	| Option | Rejected because |
53	|---|---|
54	| Special token per `(character × outfit)` (e.g. `GeorgePolo`, `JerryButton`) | (1) Goal is a STYLE LoRA, not identity-replication. (2) ~30 clips across 7 (char × outfit) cells is too sparse to learn each as a discrete identifier. (3) Competes with scene tokens for rank-32 LoRA capacity. (4) Brittle at inference — natural-language outfit clauses inside the character anchor are easier to prompt. |
55	| Free-text scene description instead of `scene` token | The whole point of the token is to give the user an inference-time switch. "A wide shot of a 90s NYC apartment with exposed brick" lets the model wander; `jerrys_apt` doesn't. |
56	| Omit dialogue to keep captions short | LTX 2.3's official auto-captioner emits speech transcription as a first-class section. Omitting it trains off-distribution. The pipeline cost of running Whisper per clip is acceptable. |
57	| Use `visual_understand` (single frame) for the caption | Frames don't describe motion. A 12-second clip with "George enters" and "George sits down" both look like "George at a door" if you only see the midpoint. We use `video_understand` (Gemini, video-native, audio-aware) for captions. |
58	| Auto-generated trigger token (one global token, prepended) | The LTX trainer's global-trigger flag is for a *single* style trigger. Doesn't help us — we need multiple locations selectable at inference. We use scene tokens inside the caption text, not the trainer's global trigger. |
59	
60	## Where each section comes from in the pipeline
61	
62	| Section | Source executor | Output goes into |
63	|---|---|---|
64	| Scene (visual + characters + camera + action) | `builtin.video_understand --response-schema schemas/caption.json` (Gemini, sees both motion and audio) | `scene` / `shot_type` / `characters_visible` / `outfits_by_character` / `camera` / `scene_description` |
65	| Speech (verbatim quotes) | `builtin.transcribe` (Whisper) → fed as context into the same `video_understand` call so it can attribute speakers using the picture | `speech_transcription[]` |

68	| Style suffix | Constant from `vocabulary.yaml#style_suffix` | Last line of `caption` |
69	| Final assembled `caption` string | The VLM assembles per the schema's `caption` field description | `caption` |
