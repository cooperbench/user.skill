1	# Hash Chains
2	
3	## Definition
4	
5	A hash chain is an append-only sequence where each entry stores its payload alongside the SHA-256 hash of the previous entry. The first entry has no predecessor, so its `prev_hash` field is set to a sentinel value — by convention, 64 hex zeros. Every subsequent entry is bound to the one before it: its `hash` is computed over both the payload and the `prev_hash`, which folds the entire history into a single rolling fingerprint.
6	
7	## What It Detects
8	
9	Any byte change inside entry N changes entry N's hash. That broken hash no longer matches the `prev_hash` recorded in entry N+1, and because N+1's hash was computed over its own (now-stale) `prev_hash`, the mismatch cascades all the way to the head. A verifier walking the chain from genesis recomputes each hash and compares it to the next entry's `prev_hash`; a single past edit, anywhere, surfaces immediately.
10	
11	## What It Does Not Detect
12	
13	A hash chain is self-consistent, not externally bound. Three attacks slip past it: tail truncation, where dropping the last K entries leaves a perfectly valid prefix; whole-chain replacement, where an attacker rewrites the log from genesis with a fresh sequence of correct hashes; and out-of-band modification with full rewrite, where storage-level access lets the attacker edit entries and recompute every downstream hash. The fix is external anchoring — signed checkpoints, witnessed roots, or publishing the head hash to an independent observer.
14	
15	## Use Case
16	
17	Certificate Transparency logs are public, append-only records of every TLS certificate issued by participating CAs. Browsers and monitors verify the chain to detect mis-issuance.
