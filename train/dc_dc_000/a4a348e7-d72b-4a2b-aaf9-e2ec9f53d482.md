Philosophically, the questions that move creative work share a shape: they **force a named answer**, not an evaluative one. "Is this good?" is useless — the maker will defend it. "Name the line you'd cut if forced" is a cut. The question has to make defending harder than answering honestly.

The deepest ones share a few moves:

- **They expose a default the maker didn't notice taking.** Defaults are where work goes generic. Form, length, opening image, tonal register — most are inherited unconsciously. A provocation that names the default forces the maker to choose it, or break it.
- **They demand a sacrifice be named.** Excellent work is shaped by what it gave up. Work that sacrificed nothing has nothing to defend and nothing to mean.
- **They locate the work uniquely in maker/moment/audience.** "Could anyone else have made this?" If yes, the work is replaceable, which is the deepest failure mode.
- **They distinguish load-bearing from decorative.** Most work hides behind ornament. The provocation has to ask which line, beat, or image would actually hurt the work to remove.
- **They surface what is being risked.** Safe work is dead on arrival. If the work isn't risking being wrong, mocked, misunderstood, or hated by someone — it's risking nothing, and nothing is what it'll evoke.

---

The actual cuts, by attack vector:

**Refusal & sacrifice**
- *Name the vow.* What is this work refusing to do? If you can't name it, the work has no spine.
- *What did you cut to get here?* If nothing, you didn't choose — you just included.
- *What's the version of this twice as committed and half as long?* Why isn't that the version?

**The first-idea trap**
- *What was your first idea?* Why isn't this it? (If it is — defend that the cliché was actually the right answer.)
- *What does this most resemble?* What stops it from being that thing? If nothing, you've made the thing it resembles.
- *Could anyone else have made this?* Name what's unmistakably yours. If you can't, you're hiding.

**Specificity & nerve**
- *Why this exact word / image / beat and not the obvious adjacent one?* For each load-bearing choice — defend the specific.
- *What does this risk being wrong about?* If the answer is "nothing," the work isn't claiming anything.
- *What lie does this tell to reach a deeper truth?* Most great creative work lies productively. Name yours.

**Engine vs. ornament**
- *What's load-bearing, and what's decoration?* Strip every flourish that isn't doing work — what survives, and is what survived enough?
- *The moment you're proudest of: is it serving the whole, or is it a darling?* Darlings are the exact thing the maker can't see.
- *What does the audience already feel coming in, and what is this teaching them to feel for the first time?* If it's only the former, you've confirmed; you haven't made.

**The unsaid**
- *Name the most important thing in this work that is never stated outright.* If everything is stated, nothing resonates. Resonance lives in the gap.
- *What does the audience have to bring?* Work that does all the work for them is condescension; work that asks too much is vanity. Where's the line you drew?

**Stakes & necessity**
- *Why this, in this form, now?* What in the moment demanded it? "I felt like making it" is not an answer.
- *Who would love this enough to steal it?* Picture them. Are you serving them, or hedging for an imaginary average viewer who doesn't exist?
- *What's at stake for someone who encounters it?* If the answer is "they'll enjoy it" — that's the bar for content, not work.

**The arc**
- *Map the experience beat by beat.* Where does it sag? You know where. Name it.
- *First five seconds: what does an indifferent stranger feel?* You don't get to assume attention.
- *The ending: does it pay for the journey, or does it just stop?* "Stops" is the most common failure of endings and the hardest one to see from the inside.

The format consideration for the brief: don't ask all of these. The brief should pick the 3-5 that most threaten *this specific work* — chosen by the form, the criterion, and what the draft is most likely hiding behind. A poem and a sketch and an ad need different cuts. The provocation engine is shared; which provocations get fired is a per-work decision.

Right — the shift is that the prompt doesn't *ask* the maker something. It hands them the knife and says "here, on your own work." Questions invite explanation; provocateurs don't explain, they cut. The brief should activate them as the adversary their work needs and probably doesn't have.

The meta-principle: every prompt is an **action against the piece**, performed by the maker, that produces evidence. Not introspection — destruction, replacement, exposure. Whatever survives, survives because it had to.

The cuts, rewritten as imperatives:

**Refusal & sacrifice**
- Write the vow this piece is keeping. One sentence. If you can't, you don't have one — give the piece a vow now or admit it's mush.
- Find the thing you would never cut. Cut it. If the piece survives, you were wrong about what was load-bearing — you were just protecting it.
- Halve it. Don't "consider" a shorter version. Write the line that survives the halving. Then ask whether the long version was hedging.

**The first idea**
- Write down the first idea you had — the one you abandoned. Put it next to this one. Defend that the current version is more dangerous than the first, or admit the first was braver and you flinched.
- Name three other people who could have made exactly this. If you can name three, you haven't made it yours — find the move that knocks two of them off the list.

**Specificity**
- Pick the three words / images / beats doing the most work. Replace each with its second-best alternative. Read the result. Be honest about whether you picked right.
- Pick the most generic moment in the piece. Make it specific to a degree that scares you. If it doesn't scare you, you weren't specific enough.

**Engine vs. ornament**
- Strip every adjective and modifier. Read what remains. The piece either survives — and your decoration wasn't earning rent — or it collapses, in which case the decoration was doing the structural job and the structure is fake.
- Name the line you'd fight hardest to keep. Cut it. If the piece is worse: you were right. If it's the same or better: kill the darling and notice you couldn't see it.

**The unsaid**
- Find a moment where you said the thing. Cut the saying. Trust the surroundings to deliver it. If it stops working, the surroundings are weak — strengthen them, do not put the saying back.
- Name what the audience has to bring to make this work. If the answer is "nothing" — you've made content, not a piece. If the answer is "everything" — you've made vanity.

**Risk & lie**
- Write your harshest critic's two-sentence takedown of this piece. Sign it as them. Now: where are they partially right? That's the place.
- Find the lie the piece tells. State it plainly. Defend that the lie is the *only* route to the truth you're after — or remove it.
- Name what this piece is risking being wrong about. If "nothing" — you've claimed nothing. Add a claim or accept that the work is decoration.

**Stakes**
- Name one specific person — real, by name, not a demographic — who would steal this. Are you writing to them, or hedging for an imaginary average viewer who does not exist? Rewrite one passage purely for the named person and notice what changes.
- Picture the smartest person you respect encountering this. They yawn. Why? Don't defend — answer.

**The arc**
- You know where it sags. Point at it without fixing it. Then say out loud whether the sag is a structural problem or a courage problem. Most sags are courage.
- Read only the ending. Does it earn the time you took? Move it to the front of the piece — would anyone still be there at the end?

**Necessity**
- Finish this sentence honestly: "This piece exists because ___." If the answer is "I felt like it," the piece doesn't have to exist, and the audience will feel that.
- Name the move in this piece nobody asked for. If there isn't one — you only delivered what was requested, which is craft, not work.

Right — the provocateur isn't only a knife. The best ones walk in carrying something the maker didn't bring: a foreign object, a stolen move, an alternate version stated with confidence, a wrong claim about the piece that's productive to refute. The brief brings *material*, not just pressure. It puts an idea on the table and dares the maker to either take it, top it, or destroy it convincingly.

A typology emerges. So far we had:
- **Cuts** — strip / expose / kill (subtractive)
- **Forces** — perform an act on the piece (transformative)

Add:
- **Sparks** — the brief itself supplies the idea, the constraint, the wrong claim, the borrowed move. The maker has to react.

Sparks are riskier — they can derail or look stupid — but a provocateur who only ever subtracts trains the maker to defend; one who throws sparks trains them to *play*. Both modes are needed.

The sparks, by sub-vector:

**Hostile gifts** — drop an image, line, or detail with attitude
- Here: a one-line image, written in the brief, that the piece doesn't have. *"There's a moment in this where someone laughs at the wrong thing and immediately knows it."* Use it, refuse it, but react.
- A line of dialogue / lyric / fragment the brief invents and asserts belongs in the piece. Maker either places it, finds where it almost fits and writes the version that does, or proves why it doesn't belong (which forces them to articulate what does).

**Substitutions — "what if it were instead"**
- What if the protagonist were the antagonist of this same scene?
- What if this happened 400 years earlier? Or in a parking lot?
- What if the most boring character were the most important?
- What if the piece were one beat shorter — and the missing beat were the climax?

**Inversions**
- The version of this that's a confession instead of a performance — write three sentences of it.
- The version told by the room, not the people in it.
- The version where the piece's central truth is wrong, and the work knows it.

**Forced transplants** — drop in something foreign and demand engagement
- Steal a structure from a different form: tarot spread, symphonic movement, courtroom transcript, recipe, wedding toast. Force this piece into it. Where does it complain? Where does it bloom?
- Insert a character who has been silent the whole time. Let them speak now. What do they say?
- Replace the medium for one beat — if it's a poem, make one line a stage direction; if it's a scene, make one beat a song lyric. What does the violation reveal?

**Borrowed moves**
- Pick a master of this form you'd never dare compare yourself to. Steal one specific move from them — name it, then deploy it. Don't worry about being them; worry about the move.
- Two pieces walk into yours: one you love, one you hate. Which moves does each contribute? The one you hate often contributes the better move.

**Smashes**
- Collide your opening image and your closing line in a single sentence. What piece is that the seed of?
- Take the antagonist and the setting and merge them. Take the protagonist's voice and apply it to the narration. What new piece emerges?

**Persona shifts**
- Imagine this were made by someone in a tradition opposed to yours. What would they have done differently in the first 10%? Steal that.
- Rewrite one passage as if you had to deliver it to your worst enemy and they had to like it. Notice what survives the diplomacy — that's structural.
- A version of this exists in the future, made by someone who knows everything you're doing. They're bored. What new move keeps them?

**Hostile claims** — assert something about the piece, possibly wrong, demand response
- *"This piece is afraid of the thing it's actually about."* Refute or absorb.
- *"The real subject of this is not what you think — it's [X]."* Where X is the brief's guess. The maker either accepts and rewrites toward X, or articulates what the real subject is more precisely than they would have without the wrong guess.
- *"You're imitating someone. I can almost name them."* Force the maker to confront unconscious mimicry.

**Generative constraints** — a cage that produces material
- Half the words are forbidden. Pick which half.
- No character can speak.
- The whole thing rhymes / is one sentence / is told entirely in questions.
- You have 60 seconds. Now 12. What's the 12-second version?
- Whatever survives this violence is the real piece. The constraint isn't the deliverable — the discovery is.

**Sensory escalation**
- What does this piece smell like? Sound like in the next room? If you can't answer, you haven't fully imagined it. Now write the line that proves you can.
- What's its temperature? What does the air feel like in it? Translate one of those answers into a concrete choice.

**The dare**
- Name the move that would get you booed. Try it.
- Name the move that would make a cynical professional nod once. Now name the move that would make a child laugh out loud or cry. Choose. Then defend the choice in the work itself.
- Write the next 30 seconds committed to your first instinct, no second-guessing. Cowardice in revision is invisible to the maker. Bypass the editor for one passage.

**Where the stance goes in the output**

Three reasonable destinations, probably all three:
- **Per-task receipt** (executor output schema) — review consumes it, plan history retains it.
- **A "director's notes" sidecar** alongside the final artifact — like commit messages for a creative work. Cheap, high-value, optional to read.
- **NOT inline in the final piece itself** — the scene/poem/ad has to stand alone. Polluting the artifact with stance commentary defeats it.

**Robustness mapping** (the interesting part)

Creative work has a different process curve than code. Code mostly improves under more rigor; creative work has a peak and then declines into over-sanded nervous mush. So robustness shouldn't just mean "more cycles" — it should mean *different angles, harder targets, more diverse provocateur voices*, with a real stopping rule.

- **Light** — single pass. Brief fires one cut + one spark (skip the force). Stance required but short — 2 sentences. No critique cycle. For sketches, throwaways, "show me an idea." Failure mode protected against: over-process killing a quick instinct.
- **Standard** — one critique/revise cycle. Brief fires the full one-cut/one-force/one-spark. Critique fires *different* provocations from a different angle (not re-litigating what was addressed). Stance required at execute and at revise — and the revise stance must explicitly say what changed and why, vs the prior stance. Review judges primary criterion + 2-3 sub-criteria + stance authenticity.
- **Robust** — multiple cycles, each with a *different provocateur voice*: e.g. cycle 1 the formalist (structure/economy), cycle 2 the iconoclast (risk/lie/dare), cycle 3 the audience (stakes/specificity for one named person). Review evaluates the full scene canvas, every beat, every sense check. For ad copy with budget behind it, performance material, the thing you'd put your name on.

The piece that's missing from current robustness for creative work: an **executor stop-affordance**. Let the maker signal "the next pass would damage the piece; stop here" with a stance defending why. Code shouldn't have this (more review = safer); creative must. Without it, robust mode reliably over-edits.

189	### Privacy & Redaction
190	
191	DataClaw applies multiple layers of protection:
192	
193	1. Username redaction - Your OS username + any configured usernames replaced with stable hashes
194	2. Secret redaction - Regex patterns catch JWT tokens, API keys (Anthropic, OpenAI, HF, GitHub, AWS, etc.), database passwords, private keys, Discord webhooks, and more
195	3. Entropy analysis - Long high-entropy strings in quotes are flagged as potential secrets
196	4. Email redaction - Regex pattern catches email addresses
197	5. Custom redaction - You can configure additional strings to redact
198	6. Tool call redaction - Tool inputs and outputs are redacted with the same standard as regular messages
199	
200	**This is NOT foolproof.** Always review your exported data before publishing.
201	Automated redaction cannot catch everything - especially service-specific
202	identifiers, third-party PII, or secrets in unusual formats.
203	
204	We recommend converting the exported jsonl into human-readable yaml using `dataclaw jsonl-to-yaml`,
205	then use tools such as [trufflehog](https://github.com/trufflesecurity/trufflehog) and [gitleaks](https://github.com/gitleaks/gitleaks) to scan it.
206	You can also compare the exported jsonl with a previous baseline using `dataclaw diff-jsonl`.
207	
208	To help improve redaction, report issues: https://github.com/peteromallet/dataclaw/issues
