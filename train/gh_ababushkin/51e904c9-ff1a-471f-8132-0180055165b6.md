## What this is about, in plain English The valuation model has a math trap. To project a company's future cash flow growth, it looks back 3 years and computes "what's the compound growth rate that would take you from year-3 to today?" That's fine for a steady business — but if year-3 was an unusually bad year, the math says the company has been growing absurdly fast, and then the model projects that fake growth rate forward for four more years. **The smoking gun:** NVDA's FY23 free cash flow was $3.8B (pre-AI). FY26 was $96.7B. The model dutifully computed a "194% annual growth rate" and projected another 4 years of 194% growth. Outcome: a "fair value" of **$7,492 per share** when the stock trades at $226. The model wasn't broken — every internal sanity check passed. The *input* was nonsense, but the input had the shape of a valid number, so nothing flagged it. Same trick fires on the simpler "pre-profit" path that uses revenue instead of cash flow: NVDA there came out at $2,246/share. Less absurd, same root cause. This isn't an NVDA problem. Any company with a weird "bad year three years ago" gets the same treatment: post-pandemic rebounds, post-IPO ramps, post-restructuring recoveries, anything that stepped up suddenly. ## What I recommend **Detection — trip the alarm if either of these is true:** 1.
